Active Recall

Instead of re-reading notes or highlighting text, actively test yourself on the material. Close your book and write down everything you remember, use flashcards, or answer practice questions without looking at your notes. Struggling to retrieve information strengthens the neural pathways associated with that memory. Passive review creates a false sense of familiarity, whereas active recall shows you what you actually know and what you need to re-learn.

Spaced repitition]

Review material at gradually increasing intervals over several days or weeks (e.g., Day 1, Day 3, Day 7, Day 14) rather than studying it all in one long session. The brain naturally forgets new information rapidly—a phenomenon known as the "forgetting curve." Spacing out your reviews interrupts this curve right before you forget the material, locking it into long-term retention with far less total study time.

Feyman Technique

Work with complete, uninterrupted focus for 25 minutes, then take a strict 5-minute break. After four cycles, take a longer 15-to-30-minute break. Long, unstructured study hours often lead to mental fatigue and distraction. Time-boxing creates a healthy sense of urgency, makes overwhelming tasks feel manageable, and prevents burnout by giving your brain regular recovery periods.

Interleaving

Mix different topics, problem types, or subjects within a single study session rather than spending hours on just one skill (blocking). For example, instead of doing 20 identical math problems in a row, mix different types of equations together. In exams and real-world scenarios, problems don't come labeled by chapter. Interleaving trains your brain to recognize which strategy or formula applies to a given problem, improving adaptability and critical thinking.

Review, review, review!

TStudy in a dedicated, clutter-free space at roughly the same time each day, and keep your phone out of reach or in another room. Every time you check a notification, it takes your brain several minutes to regain deep focus—a drain known as "attention residue." A tidy, distraction-free routine conditions your brain to enter focus mode automatically without wasting willpower.

Prioritizing Sleep Over Late-Night Cramming

Aim for 7 to 9 hours of consistent sleep, especially the night before an exam. Memory consolidation—the process where short-term memories are stabilized and stored—happens primarily during deep sleep. Pulling an all-nighter impairs your ability to recall what you already studied and reduces problem-solving accuracy.

Mind Mapping & Dual Coding

Combine words with visuals by creating diagrams, flowcharts, timelines, or concept maps that connect new ideas to concepts you already know. Your brain processes verbal and visual information through two separate channels (known as "dual coding"). Pairing concise notes with spatial diagrams gives your memory two different pathways to retrieve the same information, making complex systems and big-picture relationships much easier to recall during an exam.
